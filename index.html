From Photo to Point
How the Teachable Machine sees

From a photo to a point on a map

How a webcam frame becomes 1,280 numbers, then a dot that sits near other dots that look alike, and how that one idea runs the whole demo. Every picture on this page works: hover, press play, click the map.

photo→filters→layers→1,280 scores→point · vote→your pins→bigger picture
STEP 1

A photo is a grid of numbers

The computer never sees "a banana". It gets a grid of pixels, and every pixel is three numbers: how much red, green and blue, each from 0 to 255.

The demo shrinks each frame to 224 × 224 pixels. That's 150,528 numbers, and none of them says "banana".

Hover over the picture to read the numbers under your finger.

STEP 2

A small filter slides across it

MobileNet looks through a tiny 3 × 3 window. At each spot it multiplies the nine numbers by nine weights and adds them up. A big result means "my pattern is here".

This filter's weights look for a change from dark to light, left to right: a vertical edge. Sliding it everywhere gives a new grid, a feature map, that lights up wherever edges are.

STEP 3

Layers build simple patterns into big ones

Each layer runs many filters, and each layer looks at the previous layer's feature maps, not the photo. So the patterns get bigger and more meaningful as you go deeper.

MobileNet v2 stacks about 50 of these layers. Nobody chose the weights by hand. They were learned by training on about 1.2 million labelled photos: every wrong guess nudged the weights a little, millions of times.

Drawn by hand to show the idea. Real detectors rarely match a neat human word.

STEP 4

1,280 detectors, one score each

The last layer has 1,280 detectors. Each one leaves a small 7 × 7 feature map. MobileNet averages each map into one number: how much of that pattern is in the picture overall.

Line up all 1,280 averages and you get the picture's fingerprint, a scorecard. A banana switches on the same detectors every time, so banana scorecards look alike.

"Imagine 1,280 judges, each looking for one thing. Two pictures with almost the same scorecards must be similar things."

STEP 5

The scorecard is a point on a map

Take just two of the scores and use them as map coordinates: long curve across, round outline up. Every recorded banana lands bottom-right, every apple top-left.

A new photo becomes a new dot. The demo finds the 10 nearest recorded dots and lets them vote. That's what k = 10 means.

The real map has 1,280 directions instead of 2. We can't draw it, but "near" means the same thing: nearly the same scorecard.

Click or drag anywhere on the map to place a new photo. Arrow keys work too.

STEP 6

Putting it together at the stand

Recording: every frame becomes one dot, pinned on the map with the class name. Three seconds of holding is about 30 dots. That's all the "training" is. MobileNet itself doesn't change.

Predicting: the live camera frame becomes a new dot many times a second. Its 10 nearest pins vote, and the vote is the bars you see in the demo.

"The big network draws the map. Your examples put pins on it. A new photo just asks its neighbours."

Try the whole thing

The map starts as if a visitor has just recorded about 3 seconds of each fruit. Hold a record button to add pins, drag the blue dot (the live camera) and watch the bars. Reset empties it, like the demo.

Then try the tricks from the stand:

  • Orange: it lands near the apples, so it says Apple. It can't say "I don't know".
  • Empty view with no "Nothing" class: it still names a fruit. Record "Nothing" and try again.
  • Unbalanced: 100 extra bananas spread out and start winning votes they shouldn't.

STEP 7

The bigger picture: AI runs on maps like this

Turning things into points, so that similar things sit close, is one of the most common ideas in AI. Only the thing being mapped changes.

Big systems learn far better maps from far more data. The demo borrows a small, ready-made map and adds your pins to it.

"Same trick as your phone's photo search, just small enough to run in a browser."
